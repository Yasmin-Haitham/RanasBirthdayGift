Beyond Van Gogh · What you'll see

A guide to the works

This isn't a museum — it's a room where 300 paintings come alive around you, projected floor to ceiling. You walk through them. Here's what to look for.
Fan favorite
Saint-Rémy · 1889
The Starry Night
Painted from the window of an asylum he'd voluntarily checked into. The village below is imagined — he couldn't see one from his window. The swirling sky visually matches fluid dynamics equations that scientists only formalized in the 20th century.
Stand still and let the sky move around you. Count how many individual brushstroke directions you can spot in a single cloud.
Science fact
Arles · 1888–1889
Sunflowers
He painted 7 versions of this series for his friend Gauguin's bedroom. The chrome yellow he used is now known to darken over time — researchers confirmed this in 2011 using X-ray fluorescence. The originals were far more vivid than what we see today.
These were painted in 3 sittings each at incredible speed. Look for the difference between petals he rushed and ones he slowed down for.
No black used
Arles · 1888
Café Terrace at Night
One of his first works painted entirely outdoors at night — by candlelight, with candles literally stuck to his hat brim. He used zero black pigment in the entire painting. Darkness is built entirely from deep blues, greens, and purples.
Find where the warm yellow light meets cold blue cobblestones. That thin line is two completely different color universes touching.
Painted 3 times
Arles · 1888
The Bedroom
He painted this room three times. Colors were chosen deliberately to convey rest. He wrote to Theo: "The walls are pale violet. The floor is of red tiles. The wood of the bed — fresh butter yellow." Modern X-ray analysis found the walls were originally lilac, now faded entirely to blue.
Notice nothing in this room is perfectly straight. Every line tilts. He did this intentionally to suggest comfort — a room that has settled into itself.
Most expensive ever (1987)
Saint-Rémy · 1889
Irises
Painted one month after arriving at the asylum. Sold in 1987 for $53.9M — the highest price ever paid for a painting at the time. One solitary white iris stands alone in the violet cluster. He never explained it. It remains one of the most debated details in all of art history.
Find the one white iris. What do you think it means? He left it completely unexplained — it's yours to interpret.
Final weeks
Auvers-sur-Oise · 1890
Wheat Field with Crows
One of his final works. Three paths diverge into a wheat field — none leads anywhere. Crows fly toward the viewer. For 80 years it was called a "suicide note in paint." Then historians found a letter proving it was painted during a relatively hopeful week. Context changes everything.
It's panoramic like a cinema screen. Stand in the center of the projection. Which path would you walk?

For the curious one

The science of Van Gogh

863
Letters written
He wrote 863 letters in his lifetime — mostly to his brother Theo. Published posthumously, they're considered one of the most important art documents ever. He described colors like recipes: "a green like a warm black."
1
Paintings sold alive
Exactly one painting sold during his lifetime: The Red Vineyard. Today his works fetch $50–150M. The Starry Night is not for sale — MoMA has held it since 1941 under a condition it never be auctioned.
37
Age at death
He only started painting at 27. In 10 years he produced 900 paintings and 1,100 drawings. The pace was so intense that historians believe he sometimes completed entire works in a single sitting.
∞
Turbulence in the sky
In 2006, physicists showed the swirls in The Starry Night match the mathematical patterns of turbulent flow — a concept not formalized until 1941. Van Gogh painted fluid dynamics equations by eye, in 1889, while institutionalized.
🟡
Yellow obsession
He was obsessed with yellow. Researchers believe he may have had xanthopsia — a side effect of digitalis medication that makes yellow appear startlingly vivid. His art may literally be a record of his altered perception.
10
Years. 900 works.
He worked with such frantic urgency that he often skipped meals and slept beside wet canvases. He once wrote: "I have a terrible lucidity at moments when nature is so beautiful. I am not conscious of myself any more."
